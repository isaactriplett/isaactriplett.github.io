Background

From community college to cold plasma

Vancouver, WA → Vancouver, BC → Eugene, OR → Montréal, QC

I’m a plasma synthesis PhD candidate in Chemical Engineering at McGill University, working with Prof. Pierre-Luc Girard-Lauriault at McGill and Prof. Ahmad Hamdan at Université de Montréal. I use plasma-driven solution electrochemistry to synthesize exotic alloy nanoparticles. A non-thermal discharge in contact with the liquid supplies the reducing electrons and radicals, so no sacrificial electrode or chemical reducing agent is needed. The goal is a scalable, composition-flexible route to high-entropy alloys, which mix five or more metals in one particle. I passed my proposal defence in 2026 and expect to finish in 2028.

The work runs from simulation to the bench. I model electron transport in the discharge with Monte Carlo codes such as METHES, couple the Nernst–Planck and Navier–Stokes equations to describe the plasma–liquid interface, and run syntheses in water and in ionic liquids, then characterize the particles with STEM/EDS and XPS.

I came to chemistry through community college. After three years of chemistry, calculus and physics, I finished a BSc in Chemistry at the University of British Columbia with a focus on physical chemistry, including a year in Allan Bertram’s group studying particle-size distributions from electrospray ionization. I then joined the University of Oregon’s electrochemistry master’s internship program (MS Chemistry, 2024), working on redox-flow-battery membranes and then, in Paul Kempler’s group, on aluminum-doped hematite for electrochemical ironmaking.

Long term, I want to start a materials-synthesis company or lead advanced R&D.

Fun fact

I’ve written physics into Wikipedia.

I added an elementary derivation of the Bose–Einstein distribution from the microcanonical ensemble, and a generalization of the triple product rule. The textbook identity is

(∂x/∂y)z (∂y/∂z)x (∂z/∂x)y = −1The partial derivative of x with respect to y at constant z, times y with respect to z at constant x, times z with respect to x at constant y, equals minus one.

With one constraint on j variables, the same cyclic product equals (−1)j: +1 for four variables, −1 for five.

Interests

In the lab

Plasma–liquid chemistry
How a cold discharge over water or an ionic liquid supplies the electrons and radicals that turn metal salts into particles.
High-entropy alloys

Electron swarms
Monte Carlo electron transport with METHES and BOLSIG+, and what the Townsend model really predicts about where ionizations happen.
Statistical mechanics
Derivations a reader can follow with basic calculus, from Bose–Einstein statistics to mixing entropy in solids.
